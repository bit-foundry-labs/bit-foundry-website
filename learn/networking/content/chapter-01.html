CHAPTER 01 · LESSON

The Internet under the hood

Start with the whole path: applications create data, networks carry packets, and protocols coordinate each step.

1.1–1.3 · STRUCTURE

Edge, access, core

Applications run on end systems at the edge. Access networks connect them to routers; the core forwards packets across interconnected networks.

The Internet is a network of networks. End systems connect through access networks to routers, and providers interconnect those routers; protocols let independent networks exchange data.

In practice: A page request can cross home Wi-Fi, an ISP, transit networks, and a server network.

1.3 · SWITCHING

Packets share links

Packet switching divides messages and shares link capacity as packets arrive. Circuit switching reserves a path’s capacity in advance.

Packet switching divides messages into packets that share link capacity. Routers store packets, inspect headers, then forward them; circuit switching instead reserves capacity along a path.

In practice: Video and web traffic can share a link, but may queue when arrivals exceed its transmission rate.

1.4 · PERFORMANCE

Delay and throughput

Delay includes processing, queueing, transmission, and propagation. End-to-end throughput is limited by the bottleneck link.

dtrans = L / R

Nodal delay combines processing, queueing, transmission, and propagation. Transmission is packet length divided by link rate; propagation is distance divided by signal speed. Queueing varies with traffic.

In practice: On a slow access link, a large packet may be transmission-limited; across a long fiber path, propagation may dominate.

1.5–1.7 · PROTOCOLS

Layers and encapsulation

Each layer provides a service to the one above. Headers are added as data moves down the stack, then removed at the receiver. The chapter closes with network attacks and Internet history.

Layers divide networking into services and protocols. Encapsulation adds headers as an application message becomes a segment, datagram, and frame; the receiver removes them in reverse order.

In practice: A browser request keeps its meaning while TCP, IP, and Ethernet add delivery information.

Key distinction: Transmission delay is the time to place packet bits onto a link; propagation delay is the time those bits travel across it.
