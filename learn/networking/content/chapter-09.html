CHAPTER 09 · GUIDED LESSONS

Multimedia Networking

How networks deliver audio and video under timing constraints. Open any module for a five-page lesson with an explanation, a worked example, guided practice, and a checkpoint.

9.1 · MODULE

Multimedia applications

Audio and video traffic has timing and rate needs that differ from ordinary file transfers.

Video has high, variable bit rates; audio is continuous and timing-sensitive. Multimedia applications include stored streaming, live streaming, and conversational services, each with different delay and loss tolerance.

In practice: A movie can buffer ahead; a voice call cannot wait seconds to refill a buffer.

9.2 · MODULE

Streaming video

Adaptive streaming selects chunk quality based on bandwidth, buffer state, and playback conditions.

Stored video can stream over UDP or HTTP. HTTP adaptive streaming divides media into chunks encoded at different quality levels; the client chooses each next chunk based on network conditions and buffer state.

In practice: If bandwidth drops, a player can request a lower-bitrate chunk to avoid a playback stall.

9.3 · MODULE

VoIP and real-time audio

Real-time voice systems trade a small amount of loss tolerance for low end-to-end delay.

Best-effort IP does not guarantee delay, bandwidth, or loss. VoIP uses packetization, playback buffers, and loss recovery to balance smooth audio against conversational delay.

In practice: A larger jitter buffer smooths uneven arrivals but makes speakers hear one another later.

9.4 · MODULE

RTP and SIP

RTP carries real-time media; SIP helps establish, manage, and end multimedia sessions.

RTP carries real-time media with sequence numbers and timestamps so a receiver can reconstruct timing; it does not guarantee delivery. SIP establishes, manages, and ends multimedia sessions.

In practice: A voice service can use SIP to set up a call and RTP to carry audio.

9.5 · MODULE

Quality of service

QoS tools such as scheduling, resource reservation, and Diffserv classes can manage competing traffic.

Networks support multimedia by dimensioning capacity, prioritizing traffic classes, or reserving resources. Diffserv marks packets for per-hop treatment; admission control can reject flows when resources are insufficient.

In practice: A router may give voice low-delay service while limiting that queue so other traffic still gets capacity.
